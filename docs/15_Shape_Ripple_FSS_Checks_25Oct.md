# Shape generality, open-boundary spanning, spheroid finite-size limit and the ripple contact test (25 Oct 2026)

Scripts and raw outputs are in code/literature_checks (poly_test.py, shape_fss.py and shape_fss.jsonl, span_open.py and span_open.jsonl, ell_fss.py and ell_fss.jsonl, prolate_fss.py and prolate_fss.jsonl, ripple_mesh_test.py and its json and log, fss_analysis.py and fss_analysis.txt). Everything below is Claude's own work and has not been checked by a second person. The reviewer is asked to judge which points remain open.

## Why these runs

The thin-disc threshold of the simulator, 0.977, is 1.6 per cent above the Yi and Tawerghi (2009) value of 0.9614, and the earlier per-axis test had excluded the crossing criterion as the explanation when the box is periodic. Two questions remained. If the offset belongs to circular discs alone it would point at the disc contact test; if it is common to other plate shapes it points at something shared by the whole procedure. And Yi and Tawerghi did not use a periodic box: they put the plates in a unit cell with hard faces, counted a plate as touching a face if it crossed it, took the plate number at which half the realisations had a cluster joining two opposite faces, and extrapolated straight in the plate radius, so their procedure might differ from ours in ways that matter at the one per cent level.

## Polygon contact

Zero-thickness convex polygons are tested exactly by intersecting each polygon with the plane of the other and asking whether the two segments on the common line overlap (mode 4 of sim2e.py). For two randomly oriented plates the mean excluded volume of convex flat bodies is A P / 2, with A the area and P the perimeter. Twenty million random pairs per shape gave 11.123 plus or minus 0.008 for squares against 11.137, 12.696 plus or minus 0.012 for equilateral triangles against 12.693, and 9.870 plus or minus 0.006 for a 720-sided polygon against pi squared, so the contact test is right to 0.1 per cent for these shapes. Squares and triangles are scaled to the area of a unit circle, which is how Yi and Tawerghi define their equivalent radius.

## Squares and triangles in a periodic box

With the same estimator as for the discs (first insertion that closes a cycle winding round the box, boxes of 16 to 128 equivalent radii, 600 realisations at 16 falling to 16 at 128, the seeds in the file), the weighted mean over boxes of 32 and larger gives 0.9767 plus or minus 0.0008 for discs, 0.8794 plus or minus 0.0007 for squares and 0.7505 plus or minus 0.0006 for triangles. There is no trend with box size beyond 32 for any of them, and fits with the finite-size exponent of three-dimensional percolation give the same values within 0.002. Against the published 0.9614, 0.8647 and 0.7295 the three plateaus are 1.6, 1.7 and 2.9 per cent higher. The offset is therefore not particular to circular discs. The square to disc ratio, 0.9004 plus or minus 0.0010, equals the published 0.8994; the triangle to disc ratio, 0.7683 plus or minus 0.0008, is 1.2 per cent above the published 0.7588.

## Yi and Tawerghi's own procedure

I rewrote the percolation test with hard faces, face contact by extent of the plate along each axis, and spanning of each axis recorded separately (span_open.py; clusters carry a mask of the faces they touch, because treating each face as a shared conducting node joins unrelated clusters, which was the first draft's bug and gave nonsense). For discs the single-axis spanning count falls with box side from 1.021 at 24 radii through 1.010, 0.995, 0.990 to 0.986 at 96, and a straight-line extrapolation in the reciprocal of the box side, as in their equation 19, gives 0.9728 plus or minus 0.0019 with the mean first-spanning count and 0.9745 plus or minus 0.0019 with the median, which is the fifty per cent crossing they used; the finite-size form with the exponent 1.14 gives 0.9757 plus or minus 0.0017. For squares the two extrapolations give 0.8740 plus or minus 0.0030 and 0.8773 plus or minus 0.0026 and for triangles 0.7418 plus or minus 0.0029 and 0.7451 plus or minus 0.0026, from boxes of 24 to 64 and fewer realisations. With their extrapolation the three shapes sit 1.2 to 1.4, 1.1 and 1.7 per cent above their published values, and all three ratios between shapes agree with theirs within the errors. For squares and triangles the mean and the median give the same intercepts to 0.0003. The any-axis spanning count in the hard-faced box is flat at 0.972 to 0.977 for discs, as in the periodic box.

Reading this together: three different criteria in two different boundary conditions (periodic winding in any direction, periodic winding along one axis, hard faces with linear extrapolation) all land between 0.973 and 0.977 for discs; the choice of procedure moves the answer by at most 0.4 per cent for discs and 1.2 per cent for triangles, and leaves a common offset of about 1 to 2 per cent. I cannot say whether the remaining difference belongs to the published value or to the code. Their quoted uncertainty of 0.0005 is small beside the 0.002 that the same extrapolation gives here with 40 to 400 realisations per box, but their data are not available to me.

## Oblate spheroids in the thin limit, and prolate aspect ratio 10

With boxes of 24 to 96 equatorial radii and 200 falling to 16 realisations, aspect ratio 1000 gives a plateau of 0.9791 plus or minus 0.0012 (boxes of 32 and larger; the finite-size fit gives the same, 0.9791 plus or minus 0.0024) and aspect ratio 100 gives 0.9736 plus or minus 0.0012 (fit 0.9727 plus or minus 0.0024). The thin-limit threshold is thus 0.974 to 0.979, consistent with the cylinder and disc value of 0.9767 and 1.3 to 1.8 per cent above Yi and Tawerghi, and 23 per cent below the 1.27 that follows from Garboczi et al. (his figure is 30 per cent above ours) The earlier range of 0.976 to 0.981 from boxes of 24 and 36 radii is superseded. The 0.6 per cent step from aspect ratio 100 to 1000 is the finite-thickness effect, not a box effect.

For prolate spheroids of aspect ratio 10 the occupied fraction is 0.0681 plus or minus 0.0006 in a box of 12 long semi-axes (24 realisations), 0.0675 plus or minus 0.0004 at 24 (12), 0.0669 plus or minus 0.0005 at 36 (6) and 0.0667 plus or minus 0.0002 at 48 (3). It drifts down by 2 per cent as the box grows fourfold, which widens the gap to the 0.0724 in Table 4 of Yi and Sastry (2004) from 6 to 8 per cent, so the shortfall is not a finite-size effect of this simulator. Their neighbouring values at aspect ratios 5 and 20 agree with ours to 1.4 and 2 per cent, and the product of occupied fraction and aspect ratio is 0.685, 0.724 and 0.634 in their table against 0.675, 0.67 and 0.63 here, so their value at 10 is the one out of line with a smooth trend. I do not know the uncertainty of that table entry, and the observation does not show that it is wrong.

## The curved-sheet (ripple) contact against a mesh

The production test (sim4.sheet_contact, used for every ripple and wrinkle result) represents a sheet by a coarse square mesh of edges with a spacing of an eighth of the radius, and asks whether any edge of one sheet crosses the implicit surface of the other, in both directions, with an analytic test for the material disc. The independent test shares none of this: each sheet is a Delaunay triangulation of a hexagonal lattice plus the rim, placed on z = A sin(kx) with the arc length from the closed-form elliptic integral, and two sheets touch if any pair of triangles intersect (exact segment-triangle tests). Eighty thousand random pairs were drawn for each of six cases (flat; ripple a = 1.2; the pre-registered cases a = 2.0, wavelength 1; a = 1.5, wavelength 0.7; a = 3.0, wavelength 1.5; and a = 2.0 with a radius ratio of 0.6 between the two sheets) with mesh spacings of a twentieth and a fortieth of the radius.

The excluded volumes agree. With the production mesh at an eighth of the radius, the contact fraction is 0.4 to 0.7 per cent below the fine mesh result for the five curved cases (0.9941 to 0.9964 of the fine mesh at a fortieth), and 0.1 per cent below for flat sheets. Refining the production mesh to a sixteenth brings it to 0.2 to 0.3 per cent and to a thirty-second to 0.1 per cent or less (0.9989 to 0.9999 for the curved cases). The disagreeing pairs are almost all contacts that the fine mesh finds and the production test misses, 100 to 140 of about 18,000 contacts at an eighth, falling to 26 to 37 at a thirty-second; the production test finds a contact the mesh misses in 12 to 22 pairs whatever the spacing, which is the floor set by the chord error of the triangulated reference. The misses are intersections that form a small closed loop inside both sheets, which no mesh edge crosses. The scale-ratio case passes, so the correction of the swapped-scales bug of 22 Oct is confirmed independently.

For the percolation thresholds the consequence is a ripple threshold that is too high by about 0.4 to 0.7 per cent at the frozen setting, and the flat sheets in each paired comparison carry 0.1 per cent of the same bias, so the paired ripple-to-flat ratios are biased by 0.3 to 0.6 per cent. The ripple results were accepted within 6 per cent, so nothing needs rerunning, but the bias belongs in the error statement and the test closes item 23 for the geometry of the contact test. It does not test the arc-length parametrisation of the ripple, which is the same for both methods up to the interpolation, and it does not test thick (layered) ripple stacks.

## What follows

The 1.015 correction stays as it was (it is still the plateau ratio, and the check with squares shows that the factor is not specific to the disc). The statement for the paper is that the simulator's thresholds for three plate shapes exceed the published values by 1.6, 1.7 and 2.9 per cent in a periodic box and by 1.2 to 1.4, 1.1 and 1.7 per cent under the published procedure, with shape ratios in agreement.
